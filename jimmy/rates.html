How day, week and month pricing is built

A week is not seven days of rent. A month is not thirty.

Yards charge the most for a single day because a one-day rental carries the same paperwork, inspection and turnaround as a long one, and the machine may sit unrented the day before and after. The longer you keep it, the less each day costs. Here is what that looks like on four machines at the Sunbelt Spring Valley branch, read September 18, 2026:

Read it as: four weeks of the chipper costs the same as 8.2 single days. Across these four machines a month runs 5 to 8 times the day rate. We saw claims online of 10 to 12 times, but found no rental company's own numbers that support it.

Our own draft rate card for the operator network uses a simpler ladder: full rate for 1 to 2 days, 10% off for 3 to 4 days, 15% off a week, 25% off a month. That card is a draft and those breaks aren't set.

What counts as a day. Sunbelt's contract defines one shift as no more than 8 hours a day, 40 hours a week and 160 hours per four weeks on the hour meter. Run a double shift and the charge is 150%.

What a yard's rate does not include

The sticker price is only part of what a renter pays.

  • Insurance, or a fee in place of it. Sunbelt requires business renters to carry at least $1,000,000 general liability, plus property insurance covering the machine's full value, with Sunbelt named as additional insured and loss payee. Their contract says a policy that excludes boom damage or overturns is a breach.
  • The damage plan. Renters can instead pay Sunbelt's Rental Protection Plan at 15% of the rental charge. Sunbelt states plainly that it is not insurance and doesn't cover liability to other people. It caps what the renter owes for accidental damage.
  • Fuel and fees. The renter replaces the fuel, either prepaid, paid on return, or returned full. Sunbelt also adds an environmental fee as a percentage of the rental. The rates above are for pickup at the branch, not delivery.
  • An operator. A bare rental is the machine only. Our draft rate card treats an operated bucket truck, with Doug running it on his own insurance, as a different product at $750 a day.

So $430 for the yard's chipper is closer to $495 with the damage plan, before fuel and fees. That's the fair way to compare it to a handshake rate that has none of those extras.

The owner's side of the same day

Why the owner of the iron cares how many days it moves.

A machine costs its owner money every day whether it works or not: the loan or the cash tied up in it, insurance, registration, the annual inspections on the truck and the boom, and the value it loses each year. Then there are costs that only happen when it runs: fuel, knives, hydraulic hoses, tires, and the repair that was going to happen eventually and happened on somebody's job.

A day rate has to cover a slice of both. Rented two or three days a month, each day carries a big slice of the fixed costs. That's the honest reason single days are priced high everywhere, and why both sides usually do better when the days are predictable.
