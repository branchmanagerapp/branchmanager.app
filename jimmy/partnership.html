Second Nature Tree Service · for Doug and Jimmy

Five ways two tree outfits work together.

Same deal as the rates page: no pitch. This lays out how each arrangement works in practice. Whose customer it is, whose insurance answers when something goes wrong, who is on the hook for the iron, and what New York expects on paper.

The rules come from the state and federal agencies that enforce them, with links. Where the answer depends on a policy neither of us has read yet, it's listed as a question to ask, not stated as fact.

Nothing here is an offer or a decision

The five shapes

Most working relationships between two outfits are one of these, or a blur of two. The blur is where people get hurt.

1. Bare rental

You take the machine and run your own job with it.

Customer
The renter's. Renter's contract, renter's invoice.
Liability on the job
The renter's insurance.
Risk to the iron
The renter, by agreement. This is what rental yards put in writing.
Paper
Rental agreement, certificate of insurance, proof of coverage on rented equipment.

2. Operated rental

The machine comes with the owner's operator.

Customer
Still the hirer's job and the hirer's customer.
Liability on the job
Split. The operator is the owner's person on the owner's policy. The rest of the crew and the job belong to the hirer.
Risk to the iron
Mostly stays with the owner, because the owner's person is at the controls.
Paper
A short agreement on who directs the work, plus certificates both ways.

3. Percentage of the job

No day rate. The owner of the iron takes a share of what the job grosses.

Customer
The renter's.
Liability on the job
Same questions as a bare rental. Changing how the money moves doesn't change whose hands the machine is in.
Risk to the iron
Whatever the agreement says. It needs saying.
Paper
Everything a bare rental needs, plus a way for both sides to see the job total.

4. Subcontractor

One company sells the job. The other runs all or part of it as its own business.

Customer
The company whose name is on the contract.
Liability on the job
The prime's policy is first in line to the customer. The sub carries its own general liability and workers comp, and names the prime as additional insured.
Risk to the iron
If the prime's equipment is used on the prime's job, it's the situation the prime's policy is normally written for. Confirm with the broker.
Paper
Sub agreement, W-9, certificates of insurance for liability and comp, a 1099 at year end.

5. Employee

On the books, on payroll, for the days worked.

Customer
The employer's.
Liability on the job
The employer's policies, including workers comp for the employee.
Risk to the iron
The employer's.
Paper
Payroll, withholding, comp premium on the wages. Nothing for the worker to buy.

The insurance words, in plain English

These show up in every rental contract and sub agreement, including our own drafts.

Certificate of insurance
A one-page summary from an insurance agent showing what policies a business has, the limits and the dates. It proves coverage exists. By itself it doesn't extend coverage to anyone.
Additional insured
An endorsement that adds the other company to your liability policy for work connected to you. If a claim from your job lands on them, your policy defends them. This is the part that does the work. The certificate only reports it.
Primary and non-contributory
Your policy pays first and doesn't ask the other company's policy to chip in.
Waiver of subrogation
After paying a claim, your insurer agrees not to sue the other company to get its money back.
Rented or borrowed equipment coverage
Property coverage for a machine you don't own while it's in your care. General liability covers damage you do to other people's property on a job. It usually does not cover the machine you rented. This is a separate coverage, often sold as inland marine.

What a national yard demands, as a yardstick. Sunbelt Rentals requires business renters to carry at least $1,000,000 in general liability, plus property insurance on the equipment for its full value, with Sunbelt named as additional insured and loss payee. Their contract calls a policy that excludes boom damage or overturns a breach. Our own storm subcontractor draft asks for the same $1,000,000 liability minimum, commercial auto on every vehicle, and workers comp.

A bucket truck and a chip truck are road vehicles. That means auto insurance is in play, not only liability and equipment coverage. Whether an owner's commercial auto policy still applies when someone outside the company drives the truck for a fee, and whether the renter's auto policy covers a hired truck, depends on the wording of each policy. Neither of us should guess. It's a question for the broker.

Subcontractor or employee, in New York

The state decides this from the facts, not from what the two of you call it.

The New York Department of Labor looks at how much supervision, direction and control one side has over the other. Someone told when, where and how to do the work, and paid by the hour, looks like an employee. Someone in business for themselves, offering services to the public, free from that control, looks like an independent contractor. The Department says directly that signing an independent contractor agreement doesn't settle it. The facts of the relationship get examined anyway.

The Workers' Compensation Board weighs a list of factors for most industries, including control, a separately established business, carrying liability insurance, providing your own equipment, and signs of an independent business such as advertising, a federal tax ID and permits. One of those is worth noticing here: providing your own equipment points toward independence, and working with the other company's equipment points the other way.

For construction work New York applies a stricter test under the Construction Industry Fair Play Act, where an individual counts as an independent contractor only if three conditions are all met, and a separate business has to meet twelve. We did not determine whether tree work counts as construction under that law. That's a question for an accountant or attorney.

What a comp audit looks at

The New York State Insurance Fund puts it plainly on its subcontractor page, dated July 7, 2022: if a subcontractor doesn't have workers comp coverage, the subcontractor is included in the hiring company's policy, and the hiring company pays the premium and any claims. At audit the insurer asks for every subcontractor's certificate. If the certificates can't be produced, the premium is recalculated with those subs counted as payroll.

The one-man-shop wrinkle. A sole owner with no employees may not be required to carry comp on himself. But New York's exemption certificate, form CE-200, is only for government permits, licenses and contracts. The Workers' Compensation Board states it cannot be used to show another business, or that business's insurer, that coverage isn't required. So what a carrier will accept from a one-man sub is a question to settle with the carrier before the first job, not at the audit.

Who is allowed to run the iron

True under every arrangement above.

  • The boom. OSHA's rule is that only trained and authorized persons operate an aerial lift. Whoever employs the operator is responsible for that training. In a bare rental that's the renter.
  • The truck. In New York a single vehicle rated at 26,001 pounds or more needs a commercial driver license, Class B for a single-unit truck. The rating is on the door sticker. Worth checking on each truck rather than assuming.

Questions worth asking before choosing

None of these have been answered yet. That's the point of the list.

  • For the owner's broker: does the equipment policy cover the chipper while someone outside the company has it? Does the auto policy cover the trucks when rented out for a fee?
  • For the renter's broker: what does rented equipment coverage and hired auto coverage cost for a few days a month? Can the other company be added as additional insured and loss payee?
  • For the comp carrier: if one of us subs to the other, what proof of coverage or exemption will you accept at audit?
  • For an accountant: given how we'd really work day to day, does this look like a subcontractor or an employee?
  • For each other: how many days a month, realistically? Who pays when something breaks that was already worn? Whose customer is it next year?
