3 · Wage → bill markup (how the rate is built)

The rule of thumb that makes a subbed crew profitable:

  • Labor burden (payroll tax, WC, benefits): +25–35% on wage src
  • Overhead: +20–30% src
  • Target net margin: 10–30% src
  • Net: bill ≈ 2× to 2.5× raw wage rule-of-thumb — e.g. a $25 NY trimmer wage → ~$60–$90/hr billed at the low end; certified/climbing roles bill higher.

4 · Getting into line-clearance work — the real path

  1. Sub under a prime (Asplundh / Davey / Lewis / Clearway) — they hold the master utility contracts and hire subs for ROW work.
  2. Qualify your people under ANSI Z133 — ⚠️ being an ISA Certified Arborist is NOT enough. You need Qualified Line Clearance Arborist (QLCA) or Line Clearance Trainee status (training via ACRT or ISA).
  3. EHAP — TCIA's Electrical Hazards Awareness Program.
  4. OSHA 29 CFR 1910.269 compliance + minimum approach distances.
  5. Prequalify — TCIA Accredited Utility Contractor status + clean EMR. This is where CTSP pays off.
  6. MWBE angle — NY MWBE certification opens utility/public set-asides (Clearway has it; worth exploring if you qualify).
Path of least resistance: approach Clearway (regional, MWBE, accredited) about subbing, while you get one or two guys QLCA-qualified + EHAP. That's the realistic on-ramp — not chasing a utility master contract directly.

7 · Storm work & FEMA — the other door in

Source: Brink call, Aug 13 2026. This is the channel he actually runs, and his advice was specific: line clearance you have to be careful with — FEMA will take almost anybody on. The barrier isn't qualification, it's knowing who's holding the contract when the storm hits.

How the tiers actually work

You are not going to win a prime FEMA contract, and Brink says don't try. The money is in getting a 2nd or 3rd tier position under someone who has one, then putting crews under you.

  • Prime — holds the federal/state contract. Very hard to get in as a newcomer.
  • 2nd / 3rd tier sub — Brink's target "plenty of money to be made by putting guys under you." This is the realistic entry.
  • Crew supplier — you send trucks + guys to someone else's contract. Lowest barrier, lowest margin, best way to get known.

An SDVOB contract to prune ~32,000 street trees in Brooklyn. Paid per tree. Roughly a year of work. Brink walked away from it and the reason is the most useful number on this page:

The difference1.5 hr each way to dump
Dumping decides the margin, not the price per tree. Identical contract, identical rate, and one crew made nearly 3× the other purely on debris logistics and city traffic. Brink bailed for that reason alone — he said the Bronx version "would have been a lot easier." Before bidding any urban per-tree contract, price the dump run first. If it's over ~30 minutes each way, rework the numbers or walk.

Doug's on-ramp

  1. Tell Brink you want in on the next call-out. He offered — "as soon as a good opportunity comes, I'll turn you onto it." That's a free introduction to Brent's network.
  2. Line up crews BEFORE the call comes. The whole game is being able to say "I've got four trucks" within a day. This is exactly what the operator network is for — friends' solo tree companies, pre-agreed rates, ready to move.
  3. Write the sub agreement now. Brink: "as long as you got good contracts written up for your subs, sub-ing is a very good way to go with FEMA." Have it drafted before you need it.
  4. W-9 everyone. Every time. His words: "don't be afraid to start writing W-9s out once you start quoting big work." Paper on every dollar — no exceptions, no cash.
  5. Don't buy trucks for it. Brink's own warning: more trucks = more storage, more employees to carry in the off-season. Pass overflow to companies that already have ten trucks and take the margin on coordination.
