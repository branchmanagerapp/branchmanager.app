The rule that matters most: "commercially useful function." A certified firm has to do real work, real supervision, real management on the contract. It cannot hold the contract and sub everything to Second Nature. That is true for both Ryan's and Catherine's companies, and it is the difference between a set-aside plan and a set-aside fraud case.

Andrew's version ("2, 3 days, not that much paperwork") is optimistic on timing and silent on control. The certificate is the cheap part. The veteran running the company is the expensive part, and it is also the part that collides with TDIU below.

Ryan's rating: TDIU vs 100% schedular, and why it decides everything

On the Sept 10 call Ryan said: "it's TDIU I think it's called, where I can still physically work, I'll get less a month in disability funds, but I'm still allowed to work." His lawyer is building him a binder by next Wednesday. The two ideas in that sentence do not fit together, and which one is true decides whether this company can exist.

100% schedular
His rated conditions add up to 100% on the VA schedule. It measures how disabled he is, not whether he works. He can earn any amount, own and run any business. The plan works as described.
TDIU
Paid at the 100% rate because his conditions (rated below 100%) make him unable to hold substantially gainful employment. It is conditional on not working in a meaningful way. Running a certified company is the thing the VA looks for.

The verified rules verified

  • TDIU's standard: the veteran is unable to "secure or follow a substantially gainful occupation." Earned income at or below the one-person poverty threshold (about $16,000 a year) counts as "marginal" and is allowed; above it is gainful, unless the work is in a "protected environment such as a family business or sheltered workshop." (38 CFR 4.16)
  • Owning a business: passive income (distributions from a company he does not manage) is unlimited. Income from a business he actively runs is earned income, and the VA does not accept a low salary at face value from an owner who sets his own pay; it looks at hours, duties, and what the role would pay on the open market. (Kendall Law, Woods & Woods)
  • How they check: the annual employment questionnaire is gone; the VA runs a Social Security wage match and only sends the form when wages exceed poverty. (Hill & Ponton)
  • What happens: TDIU can be reduced after 12 consecutive months of substantially gainful work (38 CFR 3.343(c)). Falsely certifying no self-employment is a federal crime; a Moriah NY veteran got probation for it, another repaid $300k after running a construction company on TDIU. (DOJ NDNY)

The collision, stated plainly

To certify in New York, Ryan swears he "has and exercises the authority to control independently the day-to-day business decisions" (OGS). To certify federally, he swears he manages daily operations, holds the top officer role, and "generally must devote full time to the business during its normal hours" (13 CFR 128.203). To keep TDIU, he swears he cannot hold a substantially gainful occupation. Full-time control of a contracting company that wins set-asides is, on its face, a substantially gainful occupation. And the escape hatch, Ryan owns 51% while Doug actually runs it, is the veteran-figurehead pattern the government prosecutes as certification fraud (a 27-month sentence in the Padron case, $240M in set-asides). There is no version where both sworn statements are true at the same time.

The four shapes that do work judgment, from VA-accredited attorneys

  1. His rating is 100% schedular, not TDIU. Then none of this applies and he can run the company outright. This is what his own words on the call sound like ("I can still physically work, I'll get less a month"). Confirm it.
  2. Wait for schedular 100% if he is on TDIU and a rating increase is realistic. Certify after.
  3. Spouse-controlled, federal only: if his TDIU is designated permanent and total, 13 CFR 128.102 lets a spouse or permanent caregiver control the company for him. Not a friend, not a business partner. New York has no such exception.
  4. Passive investor: Ryan owns shares, takes distributions, manages nothing. Protects TDIU completely, but the company can never be certified. That is just a regular company with Ryan as an investor.

Not a shape: "marginal role, under $16k, but full-time control on the OGS form." That is the same contradiction in writing.

First question for Ryan, before any LLC gets formed: "Is your 100% schedular, or is it TDIU?" It is on his VA rating decision letter. If he does not know, the lawyer's binder must answer it on Wednesday. Nothing else on this page moves until that is known.

If Ryan dies: what survives and what doesn't

Your question was whether the contracts die with him or survive in the company. The honest answer is that the certification and the contracts are two different things, and they behave differently.

So, to your question: if Ryan builds the company, wins state set-aside contracts, and dies in year three, the New York certificate is gone within 30 days and no new set-aside work comes in. The contracts already signed most likely run to their end (each one's terms decide), and the company, crew and iron pass to whoever the operating agreement names. Federally, only a spouse keeps the status, for 3 years unless his death is service-connected or he is rated 100%. Given his liver condition, plan on the 3-year version or none. Build the company so it is worth something without the certificate, and treat the certificate as a head start, not the business.

Catherine's company · woman-owned (NYS MWBE)

What it takes verified, Sept 9 roadmap

  • 51% ownership by Catherine, with real, substantial, continuing control. She signs, she hires, she bids. Equipment can stay in Second Nature's name and be leased to her company at a fair market rate under a written lease.
  • No side letters, buyback rights, or liens against the entity. Empire State Development audits the full document set. A company that exists on paper for Doug's benefit fails certification and, if it wins work first, becomes a fraud problem.
  • Commercially useful function, same rule as Ryan's: her company must perform, supervise or manage the work, not pass it through.
  • Timing and cost: recent determinations in as few as 30 days, certification now valid 5 years, low cost. Portal: ny.newnycontracts.com; agency: Empire State Development, Division of Minority and Women's Business Development.
  • The pool: New York's MWBE goal is 30% and the state is exceeding it (32% utilization, about $3 billion a year), but that goal is combined minority and women-owned, spread across roughly 9,745 certified firms. It gets her into the bidding pool; it does not win bids.

The two cautions that came from Andrew and from you

  • Do not give Catherine 51% of Second Nature Tree. Andrew's warning, and it's right: the certified company is a new entity in her name, and Second Nature stays yours. Two companies that sub to each other, on paper, with COIs both ways.
  • The partner question is separate. "If she doesn't become partner I'm not sure this works out" (June) is about Second Nature. A woman-owned company in her name does not answer it, and mixing the two up will make both harder. Decide her stake in Second Nature on its own terms. See the crew-of-three section on the Playbook.

What the money actually is

Set-asides only matter on public procurement: state agencies, authorities, SUNY, municipalities, NYPA right-of-way work. Optimal, Servpro and Andrew's whole world are private primes hiring insured subs; no set-aside is involved, and Second Nature already qualifies for that today with GL, WC and commercial auto. That is why the Playbook puts storm work first and this page second.
