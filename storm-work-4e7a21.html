Servpro advanced 18% inside two days. Everything else was 7–9 weeks. As a Tier 2 packager with two crews on a 4-day job you need ≈ $60–80k of float for 30–60 days est. Today you have $7.4k and a $9.5k insurance bill due in eight days. ASG paid you late and then went bankrupt; you'd have eaten ~$15k if you hadn't hounded them.

Ways to cover it, cheapest first

  1. Net-45 sub agreements with pass-through of prime advances. Andrew's method in practice, but note: New York courts void "pay-when-paid" clauses (West-Fair v. Aetna, 1995), so you cannot legally make a sub's pay depend on the prime paying you. What you can do is net-45 terms, pay within 5 days when the prime's wire lands, and price the risk into the desk share. Zero cost, needs the paper in 4B and crews who trust you. Draft v1 is written that way.
  2. Prime advance. Ask for it in the rate email: "20% mobilization on day one, progress wire at day 3." Servpro did 18% without being asked.
  3. Line of credit / equipment finance. Personal credit is clean and there are no personal guarantees on old debt, but the defaulted skate EIDL is a CAIVRS hit for new SBA money. Settle the EIDL cheap first, then a bank LOC is realistic.
  4. Factoring. 2–4% a month of the invoice. Last resort, and you said you don't want it. Agreed.
Rule: no Tier 2 contract gets signed until 1 or 2 above is in place for that job, and a signed rate agreement with the prime exists before the first hour is worked (adjusters approve against the agreement, not the invoice). A $100k job with weekly payroll and a six-month wait is how tree companies die at this size. Andrew said it, the Sept 9 roadmap said it, and it is still true.

7. The deployment rule, and the crew of three

The rule, decided Sept 10 2026: when a storm call comes, residential stops. Jobs inside the deployment window get pushed, the phone still gets answered, and anyone who calls gets an estimate date for when you're back. A lost residential job is not a loss that matters next to ten days on a deployment. That part is settled, and it is not the risk.

The risk is three people on the truck. Matt's number, $3,500–4,000 a day, is for three men in a bucket. Andrew's filter for a good sub is a truck and chipper with two or three guys. You are one. The whole question is who the other two are, whether they've said yes before the storm, and who steps in when one of them can't. Right now that list lives in your head and in a group text from July.
