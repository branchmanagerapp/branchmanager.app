💸 DIY repairs vs. paying HBA — the true cost

The instinct is "fixing it ourselves is free." It isn't. When a truck or the chipper goes down in season and the crew spends the day fixing it, you still pay their wages and you lose that day's revenue. Paying a shop costs a labor bill — but the crew keeps cutting and earning. Here's the honest math.

 Fix it ourselves (DIY)Pay HBA / a shop
Crew wages that dayPaid anyway (redirected to wrench)Paid anyway (crew still cutting)
PartsSameSame
Shop labor bill$0 saved~$300–$1,000 / repair
Field-day revenueLOST (~$1,660 / day)Kept
Net hit per in-season down-day−~$1,660 revenue−~$300–$1,000 labor
Bottom line (estimate, clearly flagged): The 16 self-repair days cost roughly 16 × ~$1,660 ≈ ~$26,600 in lost revenue over 18 months (~$23k of it in 2025). Even crediting DIY with the shop labor it saved (~$600/repair × 16 ≈ ~$9,600), the net drag is on the order of ~$15–$17k — if a shop could have turned those jobs same-day. The math says: for an in-season breakdown, pay the shop and keep the crew cutting.
The honest counter-argument. DIY is genuinely free when it costs no field day — evenings, weekends, and the winter slow season (which is exactly when the smart $16k January overhaul happened). And a shop can't always fit you in same-day, so some of those lost days would've been lost regardless. The rule of thumb that actually holds: off-season & after-hours → DIY; peak-season grounding → outsource fast. October '25 (5 lost days on the loader) is the case study for what not to do.

How these numbers were built: Lost-day count = manual read of 44 in-house repair days flagged across 13,680 work texts (conservative floor — a repair could be a half-day, and untexted down-days aren't counted). Revenue/field-day ≈ $257,507 2025 revenue ÷ ~155 field days ≈ $1,661. Shop labor/repair is a rough range from HBA's actual bills ($223–$1,953 each, parts included). Treat the dollar totals as order-of-magnitude, not exact.
