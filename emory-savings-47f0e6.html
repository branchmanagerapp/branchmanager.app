For Grandpa · from Doug · November 2026

Emory's savings, and how it lasts seven generations

The goal is general savings in Emory's name that keeps growing for her children and their children, with as little as legally possible going to taxes. Here is how the two options work under New York and federal rules, so we can decide together. It's background reading, not legal or tax advice; a lawyer should look before anything is signed.

The short version

A custodial account (UTMA) is quick, free to open and simple. The money legally belongs to Emory from day one, and in New York it becomes fully hers at 21 (or 18, if the person giving chooses that when the account is set up). After that she decides how to use it.

A trust costs a lawyer's fee to set up and takes more paperwork, but the person creating it writes the rules: when Emory gets the money, in what amounts, and for what. It can keep control well past 21.

The deciding question is mostly: are we comfortable with Emory having full control of this money at 21? If yes, a custodial account is the simple choice. If no, or if the amount is large, a trust is worth the cost.

Thinking seven generations out, a custodial account can't do the job: it ends at 21. The tool built for that is a dynasty trust, set up in a state that lets a trust last indefinitely. That plan is laid out below.

Option 1

Custodial account (UTMA)

Who owns it
Emory. An adult (the custodian) manages it for her. Gifts can't be taken back.
When she gets it
Age 21 in New York, or 18 if chosen at setup.
Cost to set up
Free at most brokers. Robinhood offers them; the custodian needs their own Robinhood account.
What it can be spent on
Anything for Emory's benefit (school, medical, camp). Not the custodian's own bills or normal parenting costs.
Investments
Stocks, funds, cash. No margin or borrowing.
Option 2

Trust for Emory

Who owns it
The trust. A trustee (could be Grandpa or Doug) follows the written rules.
When she gets it
Whenever the trust says: 25, 30, in stages, or only for certain uses.
Cost to set up
A lawyer's fee to draft it, plus a tax return each year in many setups. Ask a New York lawyer for a quote.
What it can be spent on
Whatever the trust document allows.
Investments
Whatever the trustee chooses, within the rules.

A third option worth knowing about

Trump Accounts (new in 2026) are a kind of IRA for children. Family can put in up to $5,000 a year combined until the year she turns 17. Earnings grow tax-deferred. The money generally can't come out before 18, and then it becomes Emory's own IRA. Kids born 2025–2028 get a $1,000 government deposit. It's a long-term account, not general savings, but it can sit alongside either option. Details: trumpaccounts.gov.

Succession plan

Seven generations: a dynasty trust

Seven generations is roughly 175 years. Here's who it would cover:

Gen 1Grandpa
Gen 2Doug
Gen 3Emory
Gen 4–9Emory's children, grandchildren and beyond

Why New York can't host it. New York still has the old rule against perpetuities: a trust interest has to vest within the lifetimes of people alive when the trust starts, plus 21 years (EPTL 9-1.1). That's about 100 years at most, not seven generations.

Where it can live. South Dakota repealed that rule in 1983 (SDCL 43-5-8). Its remaining time limit doesn't apply when the trustee has the power to sell trust assets (SDCL 43-5-4), so a trust there can run with no end date. You don't have to live there. You use a South Dakota trust company as trustee (Delaware and Nevada work in a similar way).

How it keeps the government's share small (legally)

  1. No estate tax at each generation. Money left outright gets hit by estate tax each time someone dies, with the federal rate up to 40%. Money inside the trust isn't part of anyone's estate, so it passes from Emory to her kids to theirs with no estate tax at each step.
  2. No generation-skipping tax. Each giver allocates their federal GST exemption to the trust, which is $15,000,000 per person in 2026 and adjusted for inflation. With the exemption fully allocated, the trust is GST-exempt forever, no matter how large it grows. Gifts are reported on IRS Form 709.
  3. Funded with tax-free gifts. Each person can give $19,000 per beneficiary per year (2026) with no gift tax; Grandpa and Grandma can each do it. Larger gifts just use part of the lifetime exemption, and no tax is paid unless that exemption is exceeded.
  4. The giver pays the income tax ("grantor trust"). If it's set up as a grantor trust, Grandpa (or Doug) pays the trust's yearly income tax personally. The trust grows untaxed, and each tax payment is effectively an extra tax-free gift. That status can be switched off later.
  5. No New York income tax while it grows. A trust with no New York trustee and no New York property or New York-source income is an "exempt resident trust" and pays no New York income tax. Catch: when income it saved up is paid out to a New York resident, New York can tax that person (the throwback rule, since 2014). It doesn't apply to years before the beneficiary was born or turned 21, which is exactly when Emory's money would be growing.
  6. Protected from lawsuits and divorce. Money stays in the trust and is paid out for health, education and support, so a creditor or ex-spouse of any future family member generally can't take the principal.

Tradeoffs to know

  • No basis step-up. Assets in a trust usually don't get the capital-gains reset at death that inherited assets get. A lawyer can add a "swap power" to manage this.
  • It's permanent. It's irrevocable. A "trust protector" (a person named to update terms as laws change) keeps it from going stale over 175 years.
  • It costs money. There's a lawyer's fee to set it up and a yearly trustee fee. It makes sense once real money is going in. Until then a custodial account or Trump Account can hold early savings.

What keeps money in a family for 7 generations

Taxes are the smaller risk. Most family money is gone by the third generation because heirs spend it or fight over it. Build these in:

  • Pay out for health, education, a first home, starting a business, not as a cash allowance.
  • A letter of wishes from Grandpa and Doug saying what the money is for, read by each generation.
  • A yearly family meeting and money education starting in the teens.
  • A backup trustee and protector named for every generation.

Our recommendation: a South Dakota dynasty trust (grantor trust, GST exemption allocated) with Emory as first beneficiary, funded each year with tax-free gifts from Grandpa, Grandma and Doug. Start early savings in a custodial or Trump Account now, then move to the trust when the lawyer sets it up. This is background research, not legal or tax advice; a trusts-and-estates lawyer drafts the actual document.

Questions to bring to a lawyer
  1. Roughly how much will go in, and does that size justify a trust's cost?
  2. What age, or what conditions, do we want before Emory has full control?
  3. Who should be trustee or custodian, and who is the backup?
  4. If a trust: revocable or irrevocable, and who pays its taxes?
  5. How does each choice affect college aid?
  6. South Dakota or Delaware for a dynasty trust, which trust company, and what's the yearly fee?
  7. Grantor trust status, GST allocation on Form 709, and a trust protector: how would you set them up?
